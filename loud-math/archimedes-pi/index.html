About this activity

Squeezing π by Exhaustion

Around 250 BCE, the Greek mathematician Archimedes figured out how to pin down the value of π without ever measuring a circle directly. Instead, he trapped the circle between two polygons — one drawn just inside it (inscribed) and one drawn just outside it (circumscribed).

The circle's true perimeter must lie between these two polygon perimeters. The more sides each polygon has, the tighter the squeeze — and the closer we get to π.

The triangle decomposition

Each polygon is a stack of identical triangles meeting at the center. Archimedes used these triangles to compute the polygon's area:

Area = ½ × perimeter × apothem

For the outer polygon, the apothem equals the radius r, so its area is exactly ½ × perimeter × r. As the number of sides grows, both polygon areas converge on the circle's true area — πr².

Why 96?

Archimedes started with a regular hexagon (whose inscribed perimeter is exactly 6r) and doubled the sides four times to reach a 96-gon. From it he proved that 3 + 10/71 < π < 3 + 10/70 — the famous bound π < 22/7 still taught today, computed entirely by hand without decimals, algebra, or trigonometry.
